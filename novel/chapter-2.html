📖 Chapter 2: Saint Antoine

The streets of Paris and the meeting with Dr. Manette.

📝 Summary
Saint Antoine

Dickens showed us an image of Saint Antoine Street. In Paris, the streets were narrow and dirty, the food shops were almost empty. The faces of the children looked old already because they were hungry.

The description showed us how bad the conditions were, in order to predict future violence. Dickens hints that hunger, need, and anger will transform decent human beings into bloodthirsty animals.

Dr. Manette was unjustly imprisoned for eighteen years in the Bastille until he was considered buried in his cell. When Mr. Lorry knew that he was still alive, he told his daughter Lucie, who was really shocked by the news. The surprise was clear when she said that it would be "his ghost, not her father."

When Lucie and Mr. Lorry went to Paris to bring Dr. Manette to England, they found him old and pale. He forgot his name and only remembered the number of his cell as his name: "One Hundred and Five North Tower." He completely lost his mind but he showed two different reactions towards Lucie and Mr. Lorry. He ignored Mr. Lorry but he started the conversation with Lucie, showing that part of his mind came back to life because of his daughter Lucie.

🔍 Knitting and Shoemaking as Symbols
Knitting

Symbolizes the hidden cold-blooded unforgiving nature of the revolutionaries. Madame Defarge sat quietly knitting, appearing harmless. Similarly, the French poor people may appear simple but eventually rise up to massacre their oppressors. Knitting also symbolizes the fate of their victims — she made a list with the names of all those condemned to death.

Shoemaking

Symbolizes Dr. Manette's mental illness and a way of escaping reality. His years of imprisonment led him to forget his name and identity.

🏛️ The Effect of Imprisonment on Dr. Manette
18 Years

Mr. Lorry and Lucie Manette came to visit Dr. Manette. When Defarge asked him to tell his name to the visitor, Dr. Manette replied with "One Hundred and Five North Tower." Dr. Manette suffered a lot in the Bastille until he forgot his name. He only remembered the number of his cell in which he was imprisoned for eighteen years.
