🌟 "If—"

by Rudyard Kipling

📜 Inspirational 🏆 Nobel Prize 1907 💡 32 Lines ⭐ 1910
⬇️ Scroll down to explore the poem ⬇️
📜

Rudyard Kipling

📅 1865 – 1936

Joseph Rudyard Kipling was an English journalist, short-story writer, poet, and novelist. He is best known for The Jungle Book (1894), Kim (1901), and his poems including "If—" (1910). He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1907, making him the first English-language writer to receive the prize.

Nobel Prize Poet Novelist Journalist
If—
If you can keep your head when all about you Are losing theirs and blaming it on you, If you can trust yourself when all men doubt you, But make allowance for their doubting too; If you can wait and not be tired by waiting, Or being lied about, don't deal in lies, Or being hated, don't give way to hating, And yet don't look too good, nor talk too wise: If you can dream—and not make dreams your master; If you can think—and not make thoughts your aim; If you can meet with Triumph and Disaster And treat those two impostors just the same; If you can bear to hear the truth you've spoken Twisted by knaves to make a trap for fools, Or watch the things you gave your life to, broken, And stoop and build 'em up with worn-out tools: If you can make one heap of all your winnings And risk it on one turn of pitch-and-toss, And lose, and start again at your beginnings And never breathe a word about your loss; If you can force your heart and nerve and sinew To serve your turn long after they are gone, And so hold on when there is nothing in you Except the Will which says to them: 'Hold on!' If you can talk with crowds and keep your virtue, Or walk with Kings—nor lose the common touch, If neither foes nor loving friends can hurt you, If all men count with you, but none too much; If you can fill the unforgiving minute With sixty seconds' worth of distance run, Yours is the Earth and everything that's in it, And—which is more—you'll be a Man, my son!
— Rudyard Kipling

💡 Main Idea

A father advises his son to prepare himself to face life with its good and bad sides. If the son follows his father's advice, he will own the world and everything in it. Moreover, he will be a man in the full sense of the word.

📖 Stanza 1

Self-Control & Patience

Stanza 1 Self-Control
If you can keep your head when all about you Are losing theirs and blaming it on you, If you can trust yourself when all men doubt you, But make allowance for their doubting too; If you can wait and not be tired of waiting, Or being lied about, don't deal in lies, Or being hated, don't give way to hating, And yet don't look too good, nor talk too wise:

📖 Vocabulary

keep your head be calm and brave blaming you are responsible; your mistake doubt don't believe; don't trust allowance understanding

📝 Paraphrase

This poem is a list of advice that a father gives his son. In a bad situation, it is important to stay calm even if the people around you panic and say you are responsible for this mistake. It is important to trust yourself even if other people mistrust you and to accept other people's criticism patiently. Don't lie to people who lie to you and forgive those who hate you. The father advises his son to be modest and not to behave in a way that shows he is better or smarter than others.

🔍 Literary Terms

  • Antithesis (Contrast): The entire stanza is based on antithesis — keep your head × losing theirs; trust yourself × doubt you; lied about × don't deal in lies; being hated × don't give way to hating
  • Alliteration: "Or being lied about, don't deal in lies" — the consonant sound 'd' is repeated in 'don't' and 'deal'.
  • Repetition: "Or being" is repeated twice in lines 6 and 7.
  • Assonance: "And yet don't look too good, nor talk too wise" — the vowel sound "o" is repeated in "look" and "good".
  • Metaphor: "If you can keep your head when all about you" — "keep your head" is a metaphor for staying calm and cool.

📖 Stanza 2

Dreams, Reality & Resilience

Stanza 2 Dreams & Reality
If you can dream—and not make dreams your master; If you can think—and not make thoughts your aim; If you can meet with Triumph and Disaster, And treat those two impostors just the same; If you can bear to hear the truth you've spoken Twisted by knaves to make a trap for fools, Or watch the things you gave your life to, broken, And stoop and build 'em up with worn-out tools:

📖 Vocabulary

master leader; controller aim goal; reason triumph success; victory disaster failure impostors deceivers; cheaters twisted changed knaves evil and bad people stoop to bend over from being so tired

📝 Paraphrase

The father advises his son to be realistic and rational. It is good for the son to imagine the future, but should not let the dreams have control over him. Son should think with an open mind, but not make thinking his goal. Both success and failure are deceivers, so they should be treated the same. Son should be tolerant towards evil people who try to change his words just to fool naïve people. Son should not be desperate when he sees all the things he has achieved lost or destroyed, but should try hard to get them back, using previously exhausted tools (skills).

🔍 Literary Terms

  • Metaphor: "If you can dream—and not make dreams your master" — dreams are compared to a person in control. "And stoop and build 'em up with worn-out tools" — the skills of life are compared to 'worn-out tools'.
  • Alliteration: "And treat those two impostors just the same" — the consonant sound 't' is repeated in 'treat' and 'two'. "Twisted by knaves to make a trap for fools" — the consonant sound 'f' is repeated in 'for' and 'fools'.
  • Antithesis: "If you can meet with Triumph and Disaster" — 'Triumph' and 'Disaster' are opposites.
  • Repetition: "If you can" is repeated four times in lines 1, 2, 3, 5.
  • Assonance: "And treat those two impostors just the same" — the vowel sound "o" is repeated in "those" and "impostors".

📖 Stanza 3

Perseverance & Willpower

Stanza 3 Perseverance
If you can make one heap of all your winnings And risk it on one turn of pitch and toss, And lose, and start again at your beginnings And never breathe a word about your loss; If you can force your heart and nerve and sinew To serve your turn long after they are gone, And so hold on when there is nothing in you Except the Will which says to them: "Hold on!"

📖 Vocabulary

heap a pile risk danger; a threat turn of pitch and toss a game of chance; luck sinew muscles; tendons hold on don't give up or surrender

📝 Paraphrase

The advice continues. It is good if son can save his money, but should not be afraid to take a chance at a game of luck and then lose. To lose is only a new beginning. Start again, but never tell anybody that you lost. Don't complain or lose your temper even if you are tired, but keep trying and use your strength to start again. The father advises his son to be brave and to have faith in his heart and will.

🔍 Literary Terms

  • Alliteration: "Except the Will which says to them: 'Hold on!'" — the consonant sound 'w' is repeated in 'Will' and 'which'.
  • Personification: "Except the Will which says to them: 'Hold on!'" — 'Will' is personified and given the human ability to talk.
  • Assonance: "And so hold on when there is nothing in you" — the vowel sound "o" is repeated in 'so' and 'hold'.
  • Repetition: The word "And" is repeated several times at the beginning of lines.

📖 Stanza 4

Virtue & Success

Stanza 4 Virtue & Success
If you can talk with crowds and keep your virtue, Or walk with Kings—nor lose the common touch, If neither foes nor loving friends can hurt you, If all men count with you, but none too much; If you can fill the unforgiving minute With sixty seconds' worth of distance run, Yours is the Earth and everything that's in it, And—which is more—you'll be a Man, my son!

📖 Vocabulary

crowds common and simple people virtue goodness; honesty common touch to understand people — rich, poor, everyone foes enemies

📝 Paraphrase

The advice continues. Son should treat all people equally, whether poor or rich. Son's behaviour and attitudes should show high moral standards when he deals with both simple, poor people and high ranking men in authority. As a result, neither friends nor enemies would be able to injure him. He should try to compensate for every minute of hatred with fruitful services and labour. In the last two lines, the father tells his son that if he can comply with all this advice, he will inherit the Earth and truly be a man.

🔍 Literary Terms

  • Antithesis (Contrast): "If you can talk with crowds and keep your virtue, / Or walk with Kings—nor lose the common touch" — contrast between 'crowds' and 'Kings'.
  • Metaphor: "Yours is the Earth and everything that's in it" — the Earth symbolizes success and achievement.
